Manifesto

Position paper. On the responsible use of memes in scientific talks.

Abstract

Scientific talks tend to lose the room somewhere between the related-work slide and the second equation. We argue that a well-chosen meme, placed with intent and used sparingly, wins the room back, reminds the audience that the speaker is a person, and makes the point easier to remember. A badly chosen meme does none of these things. This journal exists to make the first case more common than the second.

Keywords: memes, attention, slide 4, Reviewer 2, open licences

References (real ones)

Banas, J. A., Dunbar, N., Rodriguez, D., & Liu, S.-J. (2011). A review of humor in educational settings: Four decades of research. Communication Education, 60(1), 115–144.

Bradbury, N. A. (2016). Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more? Advances in Physiology Education, 40(4), 509–513.

  1. I

    Humour is a tool, not a distraction.

    Decades of education research associate appropriate, on-topic humour with attention and a better classroom climate, and associate off-topic or hostile humour with the opposite (Banas et al., 2011). The operative word is appropriate.

  2. II

    Attention is finite. Spend it on purpose.

    The famous ten-minute attention span is mostly folklore (Bradbury, 2016), so we claim nothing that precise. We claim only that a room which just laughed is a room which is looking at your slide.

  3. III

    A meme is a compressed argument.

    A good meme carries a claim the audience already knows how to read, so the setup costs zero seconds. If it needs explaining, it is a figure, and figures need axes.

  4. IV

    Punch up, never down.

    Mock the hardware, the deadlines, Reviewer 2, the field's buzzwords and yourself. Never students, never a named colleague, never a group of people.

  5. V

    The point outranks the laugh.

    If deleting the meme removes nothing but a chuckle, delete it. The best ones survive because the slide makes its argument through them.

  6. VI

    Credit is not optional.

    We cite the papers we build on; the pictures deserve the same. Every image here is public domain or openly licensed, and every download says where it came from.

Dosing guidelines

Methods section. Follow these and the room laughs with you. Ignore them and the room checks email.

Dose

Keep meme density low

μ = memes / slides ≤ 0.1

One meme every ten slides or so. Two in a row means the talk has become a meme with slides.

Legibility

The three-second rule

If the back row can't read it in three seconds, it is a figure, not a meme. Two short lines at most.

Placement

Use it at a transition

Openings, the slide after the hardest equation, limitations and Q&A. Every meme here is tagged with the part of the talk it was made for.

Audience

Know the room

A joke that lands in a lab meeting can fall flat at a keynote. When the room includes people who don't know you, keep the target yourself or the hardware.

Accessibility

Say the caption out loud

Not everyone can see the slide. Read the text as you show it and give the image alt text in your slides.

Attribution

Keep the credit line

Downloads come with the image credit in the corner. Leave it there. It costs nothing and it is the reason this library can exist.

Assumes about one slide per minute and μ ≈ 0.1.

Submit a meme

The library grows by submission. Make your meme from your own photo or an openly licensed image, say where the image comes from, and send it in.

  1. Draft it here

    Pick your finished meme image and fill in where the picture comes from. The preview shows how it will look in the gallery.

  2. Open the submission on GitHub

    The button opens a prefilled submission form. You need a free GitHub account.

  3. Drag your image into the form

    GitHub stores the image with the submission. This page never uploads anything itself.

  4. Peer review

    A maintainer checks the joke and the licence, then adds the accepted label.

  5. Publication

    A bot adds the meme to the gallery with its credit, and it appears on the site a few minutes later.

Good places for openly licensed pictures: Wikimedia Commons, NASA image libraries, and museum open-access collections. Only use images whose licence allows reuse.
